**JUNE:** April 20th, and I'm talking to A.J. Patterson. J.D. Patterson. J.D. Patterson, I'm sorry. And he was telling about his teaching.

**JD:** Well, I went to Taylorstown then after completing that semester at California. And of course it was only a fourth class district only had eight months school at that time and our salaries were a hundred dollars a piece and no ifs and ands about it no matter what grades you taught

**JD:** This was a hundred dollars a month a hundred dollars a month and just the eight months that you taught eight months eight hundred dollars a year and your retirement was taken out of that well the third year we were there we were informed that we would have to give back $10 a month. So that amounted to $720. In other words, $90 a month for eight months, those two years. Then the last year I was there, they didn't take any or didn't give any back. So we had our $100 a month again. Then when I went to Canton Township, which takes in West Washington, Wolfdale,

**JD:** It was a third class district. And the salary there was $1,000 a year with $100 increment each year until you worked up to $1,400.

**JUNE:** What's increment mean?

**JD:** Increase.

**JUNE:** Oh, uh-huh. All right.

**JD:** So I was there seven years and taught departmental. And while I was there, I completed my work at California and got my degree. Then when the war came on, I went down to Chartier's Township and taught in the high school there three years.

**JD:** Pardon me.

**JD:** The salaries had got up to

**JD:** I think 1650 or something by that time, still a very pittance.

**JD:** Well, at the end of the three years then, I didn't go back. See, I didn't have a permanent contract there because I went as a substitute for people on military leave.

**JD:** So then I went west again to make a visit in the early part of 47.

**JUNE:** To Nebraska?

**JD:** To Nebraska, and I intended, you know, to come back in time to apply for school, but when I got out there, they froze onto me and practically drafted me into a teaching position.

**JD:** It was a delightful experience. I taught all four classes of English and one class in public speaking in a high school called Wood River, Nebraska.

**JUNE:** What year was this about?

**JD:** \1947.

**JUNE:** Uh-huh.

**JD:** There was one full section of each of the 9, 10, 11, and 12 grades, and they had, for the first time in the history of that little town, they had a senior prom that year. Some of the old residents had been some kind of Methodist or Lutherans or something who didn't approve of dancing, so they'd never been allowed to have dances in the school.

**JD:** Until that spring they had a couple of school dances and then they had the senior prom

**JD:** Well uh I was offered to take charge of the schools in a smaller village the next year so I was superintendent of the Alda schools the winter of 47 and 8

**JUNE:** In Nebraska?

**JD:** Yeah.

**JUNE:** I bet you — you noticed a big difference in Nebraska from when you were there as a child.

**JD:** Well, it was a different part of the state too. This was down in the Platte Valley. And of course the salaries were better there than here uh I don't remember what I got the spring at Wood River but this next year I got 3 000 and I've been getting 1650 Chartiers so that was half again as much yes so I was very happy to stay on I sent to Harrisburg and got my credits sent to Lincoln and got a Nebraska certificate so I took charge of the Alda schools for that year then the next year, the superintendent, the new superintendent was elected at Shelton, which is 20, 23 miles west of Grand Island. He came down to Alda and wanted to know if I would take the English classes and act as principal of the Shelton High School the next year. So I went up there and was there four years.

**JD:** And then my certificate was good for five years. So that made it, it was used up the year at Alda and the four years at Shelton. So rather than renew it, I thought I'd better come back to Pennsylvania on the count of my years here to get my retirement in Pennsylvania. So I came back and I landed in Peters Township and I was under contract there 14 years. Then I retired, but I'd had two years leave and was over in West Virginia two years. Then when I retired in Pennsylvania, I went back to West Virginia and taught three more. So I got five years in West Virginia so I have a small retirement from there too

**JUNE:** When — when you went to college, do you remember how much it cost to go to college in those days?

**JD:** The tuition was almost free it was just a token amount and the room, board and laundry was eight dollars a week.

**JUNE:** Uh-huh. And how much — you didn't have to pay for each course you took?

**JD:** No, no. You paid a nominal fee when you registered.

**JUNE:** Like $5, $10?

**JD:** It runs in my mind, it was about $24 or something like that altogether. You had a contingent fee and a breakage fee, and some of that was returned to you in the spring, if you didn't break anything.

**JUNE:** Oh.

**JUNE:** And then I was thinking — when you taught in Chartiers, which school did you teach there?

**JD:** There, which... It's the same one. It's called Chartier's Houston now.

**JUNE:** Oh.

**JD:** But that was before the jointures. During the war years, it was just Chartier's Township High School.

**JUNE:** Oh.

**JD:** Right there where the school still is. They've added to it, but it was the same original building.

**JUNE:** Do you remember anything special, anything about the children? Did they seem eager to learn in those days, the early days? Or were they...

**JD:** Yes, teaching was much easier then in a way than it is today.

**JD:** You didn't have the discipline problems. And I was very fortunate, I guess. I got located in places where things were. Now, the Taylorstown children, for instance, they were so nice. It was a delight to work there.

**JD:** And at Chartier's,

**JD:** Well, I should say — Hayes Avenue next because that's where I went from Taylorstown most of those kids if you showed you had a heart and was a little bit nice to them they appreciated it and they had so little at home that school was kind of nice for them and

**JD:** At Chartiers it was a little more modern and, well, of course, they had liked that when the school was new, it had only been four years since they opened the school. They had a lot of young fellows who had come out of college, and they had some very, I would say, popular teachers. Well, of course, they were draft material, so they all went off to war, and they kind of looked down their noses at some of us who came in then to take their places. But they got used to it and were nice enough.

**JUNE:** Do you know any difference between the school as you finished than when you started teaching school, like the courses? What courses were you teaching at the end in high school? Were you teaching English, did you say, in history?

**JD:** When I came to Peters, there weren't too many in the senior high school. The district hadn't begun to build up quite so much then. The first year I was there, I took a seventh grade room. Then the second year, a departmental seventh and eighth. Then the third year, I was moved up into the high school and I taught POD in American history and ninth-grade English.

**JUNE:** What's PLD?

**JD:** Problems of democracy.

**JUNE:** Oh, POD.

**JD:** Uh-huh. And then I taught uh one year I taught ninth grade Pennsylvania history and civics I never did teach any 10th grade subjects there was just kind of a coincidence that never happened that my schedule included a 10th grade subject but I taught English in the other years 11th grade junior English and American history, world geography — they put that in as an elective.

**JUNE:** Oh, back about 1950, eight or nine? Was it a popular subject?

**JD:** Yes. One year we had a good sized section; the second year we had two sections.

**JD:** And so I had to cut down on my history, because I was the only one that actually had a certificate that specifically said geography and secondary.

**JD:** Some of them had social studies, which would have covered it, but I was the only one that actually had geography mentioned.

**JUNE:** How many children did you have in a class there at Peters Township?

**JD:** Oh, from 25 to 35.

**JUNE:** And were they easy to manage?

**JD:** Yes, the kids at Peters were very nice. Wasn't too large of a classroom.

**JD:** You know, like, I know sometimes when the classrooms get up to about 50 children, it's very hard. The biggest class I think I ever had was one year at Hayes Avenue. We had 48 in the eighth grade. And that really should have been two sections because you can't teach 48. I asked one of the education men at the college. He said no. I said, no — now just what would you do in a case like that? Well, first I think you better pray a little.

**JUNE:** Yes.

**JUNE:** You said this Hayes Avenue — was this Hayes Avenue school, that was the name of the building? Whereabouts was that? What township — Canton Township?

**JD:** Oh, Canton Town. It's part of the Trinity Area schools today. You've heard of that, haven't you?

**JUNE:** Yes, yes.

**JD:** Well, when you go out West Chestnut Street out of Washington to go out Route 40, the last stoplight before you leave the city is Hayes Avenue. At that time, you went right up there and down over to the school. But since that, Route 70 has come through there, and Hayes Avenue is cutting, too. You can't get across it. So you have to go way up here and back down around and it's no longer called from that new part there that the road kept into

**JD:** Beyond that, it's called just a place now. It's no longer called Hayes Avenue.

**JD:** But up to, from Route 40, West Chestnut Street, over to where that road cut through, it's still Hayes Avenue.

**JUNE:** All right, do you want to tell about the Bethel Center?

**JD:** In 1916, when we came back from the West, there were two rooms down and a large room over those two upstairs that could have a curtain put across it and divide if necessary. But they usually used it as it was for a classroom. And they had two high school teachers and a seventh and eighth grade teacher. Downstairs as you walked in from Park Avenue was it's closed off and it's a solid wall now but that was the front doors and uh when you went in there was a cloakroom on the left and then just back of that or straight ahead was the seventh and eighth grade room and on the right was the stairway to upstairs and then the door by that went into the high school room and then the other high school room was upstairs in the auditorium which was the big room that covered the two downstairs rooms for those three teachers Mr. Hildy was principal and teacher in the high school and Miss Hazlett and Miss Hubbard taught the two grades seventh and eighth

**JD:** Then that was the winter of 1916 and 17. Well war was declared in April 1917. And in the meantime, the school board had been petitioning the state to have a vocational school started there. So they started plans to add, as soon as school was out that spring, to add to the building. And they built on.

**JD:** Behind these three little rooms made that building as it is today. It's now the Schoolhouse Arts Building.

**JD:** So they had on the first floor, they had home ec.

**JD:** They didn't call it that then. Girls vocational but they taught cooking, sewing and house care and all that then the basement had the shop wood shop and the class to teach farming subjects now we had vegetable gardening and poultry and shop and mechanical drawing in freshman year and the sophomore year we had field crops and forestry and surveying and shop

**JD:** In the third year, we had orcharding and dairying and shop. In the fourth year, we had farm mechanics, farm law, farm management, and rural sociology and shop. Now, the first and fourth years, our vocational work was pretty good. But the second and third years, we had a teacher who didn't care much for teaching.

**JD:** He just wouldn't bother preparing lesson plans on all the TITSO subjects we should have had more work in.

**JD:** The state schedule called for the afternoon to be divided into four periods.

**JD:** Two for the subjects like field crops or whichever year you were in, and the other two periods for shop.

**JD:** But we had all afternoon, that was twice a week. Tuesday and Thursday. We had shop all afternoon Tuesday, all afternoon Thursday, and then there wasn't supposed to be any on Friday, but we'd have a little bit of classwork, and then we'd go over in the shop. So it was most of Friday, too. And I bitterly hated shop.

**JUNE:** What did they do in shop?

**JD:** Oh, we made things, and we built the cement walks, and we repaired the Mine 3 school, Phillips School at Mine 3, and mechanical drawing. Of course, mine was always a mess. I was not a bit artistic. So I hated everything except the classwork and vocational, the field crops and the dairying and orcharding and barn management. I liked that.

**JUNE:** When you said — like you did the sidewalk over at the Phillips School, you left the classroom, you went outside and worked.

**JD:** And he left the ones that weren't going on that project with some one of the older boys in charge and worked for us to do in the shop

**JUNE:** What was this Phillips School, you said? Was it at Mine Number 3?

**JD:** Yeah, Mine Three.

**JUNE:** Where was Mine Three? Was it in Bethel?

**JD:** You know where Washington Junction on the car line is, where the car divides. One goes to Drake.

**JUNE:** Oh, yes, yes, yes.

**JD:** Right over from Washington Junction, that area around there was Mine Three. The Phillips School was over on that hill there, right just across from Washington Junction on the car.

**JUNE:** Oh, well.

**JD:** They had three rooms, I think. I don't know how it was arranged whether it was two up and one down or one down and two up or what it was. It's probably Castle Shannon now.

**JUNE:** Isn't that Castle Shannon?

**JD:** No, it's in — it's in Bethel it's part of Bethel oh there's no school there now I suppose the little ones go to Hillcrest, and the other ones go to wherever it is. At any rate, I graduated from Bethel in 1921, and it was, well, during my last couple of years there, and then from then on, Bethel began to grow. When I was in eighth grade, there were two graduates from the high school. Two children.

**JD:** Bill Lytle and Doug McElvain graduated in 1917. In 1918 there were two graduated uh Marie Boyer and Cecil Bowles in 1919 there were six graduated there were four girls Blanche Wichas, Jesse Holly, Grace Boyer, and Grace Kale, two boys, Joseph Phillips and Vernon Mullenauer.

**JD:** 1920, I believe there were eight,

**JD:** Was Alda Bates, Mary Simmons, Alta Thompson, and Helene McElvain.

**JD:** Philips Boyer, Leo Molinar, Bob Lytle was in that class, but I don't think he graduated.

**JD:** Then in our class, there were 12. They're getting larger. Eight girls and four boys. Four boys were all still living. Richard Stute, Carl Haas, John Trax and I and there's one other girl, Sarah Lockman is gone she died in '49 of cancer but the other girls I believe we're all still living.

**JD:** Then from then on it began to get larger.

**JUNE:** And you talked about the vocational subjects — and did you just have those in the afternoon? Like, when did you have your English and history and math, in the morning?

**JD:** Well, the freshmen, ninth grade, had their vocational in the forenoon, and academic in the afternoon. The other three years, 10th, 11th, and 12th, were always combined in vocational. So they had their academic subjects in the forenoon, and their vocational in the afternoon.

**JUNE:** And what major subjects did you have? English, math?

**JD:** Well, I had algebra and physical geography, four years English, American history. I had a semester of civics and a semester of biology.

**JD:** And first year French.

**JUNE:** Oh, uh-huh. Did they teach any other languages? Did they teach German or Latin?

**JD:** They had had German the year I was in eighth grade, but they cut it out on the account of being at war with Germany the year I was in ninth grade, and they put in French instead. And they also had Latin. Now, there weren't very many who took Latin, but there was a Latin class.

**JD:** I'm not sure whether there was one my freshman year. I know there was a Latin class my sophomore year.

**JUNE:** Thinking about the war, did you go off to the war, World War I?

**JD:** No, I wasn't old enough.

**JUNE:** Any of your friends?

**JD:** Yeah, I was only 17 in 1921.

**JUNE:** Oh, oh, uh-huh. Do you remember the boys going off?

**JD:** Very well.

**JUNE:** Uh-huh.

**JD:** Yes, we had a lot of things going on at the high school to help to sell Liberty Bonds.

**JD:** Patriotic in general, and I loved the World War I music over there, Keep the Home Fires Burning and the Long, Long Trail.

**JD:** It had a lot of rhythm.

**JUNE:** Yes.

**JD:** There was another one or two, I can't just think of the titles right now.

**JUNE:** Then, when the boys came home, did you have any close friends that had gone to war?

**JD:** Well, some of the Walther boys, J-5, Donald McKeown had been in.

**JUNE:** Did you know the McKeown family?

**JD:** Yes, my father's sister married Alan McKeown. They had six children. They were big cousins of mine.

**JD:** There's a Roy McKeown still living.

**JUNE:** Was he Kiefer's son, I believe?

**JD:** Yeah. Alan McKeown was my uncle by marriage. There was John and Kiefer and another one or two.

**JUNE:** Now, Roy — was he the youngest brother, or was he the next generation?

**JD:** I think he's a brother. I think he's a brother. Maybe.

**JUNE:** He'd be very old then.

**JD:** Yes.

**JUNE:** And there was a Blanche McKeown.

**JD:** Yes. She taught Sunday school.

**JD:** I don't know. They lived in Castle Shannon.

**JUNE:** Oh.

**JUNE:** I've heard of him.

**JD:** Yes. Let's see. Well, the Orr farm was taken in by that, uh, Trotwood development.

**JUNE:** Uh-huh. Trotwood Acres, they call it, don't they?

**JD:** I don't know. There's several Trotwood. There's Trotwood West and North and Trotwood Acres. You're right. Yes.

**JD:** Not too much at that time where the car turns to get out and make the loop just ahead of that on the left was an old wooden eight room school building and there was swings and things in the yard around there. You wouldn't believe it's the same place today, but I can remember it very well. And if you go in along from the Beverly Heights Church, between that, well, all along there on the right going in, I remember when an Italian family had a big farm there and had that all plowed up and raised tomatoes and cabbage and market gardening, you know, truck farming.

**JUNE:** Uh-huh. This was back when you were growing up?

**JD:** Yes, yes.

**JUNE:** Was he?

**JD:** Anna, and I think there were a couple of others I didn't know them after that

And of course it was daylight when they got home or after way after probably but they got the wharf market came late at night instead of early in the morning the fellows come in and so bob still they had gone in I suppose about dark with his load they had a truck by that time and he returned about midnight and when he pulled into the yard and got out of his truck he was shot here in, uh — off of Fort Couch?
