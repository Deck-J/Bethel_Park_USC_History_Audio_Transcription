**ANN:** As far as we have been able to know, we really didn't get very far. We only scratched the surface as far as the history of our church and of the Presbyterian Church in Western Pennsylvania. But I hope that out of these classes we can have a deeper appreciation of the life that our frontier forefathers lived in this area, their way of life, their tremendous interest in religion, and also their interest in education.

**ANN:** This is one of the things that I've tried to emphasize through each session, that the Presbyterian forefathers were interested not only in their own religion, but in educating their children and providing for them the opportunities to go on to a better life.

**ANN:** And we talked about George Marshall, especially before, and how he was influenced by very fine teachers in his early life, and through that established that special academy as an early part of his activity in relationship to Bethel Church, because it has been significant to me that this church has survived because of its contacts and its influences by other churches.

**ANN:** Last week, we sort of left the church and went elsewhere, took the group and went to Mingo Presbyterian Church, which enabled us to touch hands with history of the past, and the relationship that Bethel Church had with the Mingo congregation during the period [unclear].

**ANN:** This week we're going to touch in again with another congregation.

**ANN:** This time with the history of Cross Creek Presbyterian Church, which very definitely was interwoven with our own history during the pastorate of Dr. George Marshall. And the one man that I think can really open this picture to us, here with us today, to give us his history of the Cross Creek Church, and also some of the other materials that he can so ably provide for us as background.

**ANN:** Before I introduce him, I would like to state that one of the most important things that Dr. Marshall considered to the life of the church was his library. He loved books, and he spent most of his money, what little he had extra, in accumulating an outstanding library. And he lamented the fact that so many churches spend so much money on the beautification of their church and so little to provide books for the minister who needed that for research and study.

**ANN:** We have with us a man who also loves books, who has written a history of the Cross Creek Church, has an extensive library of his own, and is most gracious in sharing with us some of the books that are precious to him. And because we have a fine library, I hope all of you will take advantage of it. Under the supervision of Mrs. Tanner, I'm going to present to her the book which Mr. Alvin Dinsmore White made and has put in our library, and in the front has inserted the following.

**ANN:** This volume, History of the Presbytery of Washington, is presented to the library at the historical collection of the Bethel Presbyterian Church by Alvin Dinsmore White of Hickory, Pennsylvania, in loving memory of his great, great, great grandfather, James Dinsmore, a noted churchman and once an elder in the Bethel Presbyterian Church. He was one of the first three recorded in our history at the time that the church was still meeting at the log cabin home of Oliver Miller.

**ANN:** And then it goes on to say that there is information here concerning James Dinsmore, 1742 to 1817, served as elder in Bethel Church until 1795; John Dinsmore, 1779 to 1858, served as elder for many years in Upper Buffalo Church; James Dinsmore, 1806 to 1870, who served as elder in the Cross Creek district.

**ANN:** Thank you. I hope that you will make that book available to us through the library. You might be interested to know that Mrs. Moser, in her fine talk on revivalism in western Pennsylvania — use them, they're quite accessible for her. We have a few other books on the shelf that are referred to in this book, so that we'll get things together, so that you can find them wherever we put them.

**ANN:** Thank you. Thank you very much. And now, without any further speech or debate, to you, Alvin Dinsmore White, who has a long history and love and interest in the Presbyterian Church. He grew up as a boy in the Cross Creek Church, was an elder in the [unclear] Cross Creek Church, and also was not only a churchman but an educator. He taught school, beginning in a one-room schoolhouse, then he taught in a high school, and then became supervisor of elementary education for various districts in the Avella school district.

**ANN:** He is an author, a historian, a teacher, and a devoted churchman.

**WHITE:** Thank you, Mrs. Conner. After such an introduction, I wish I could bring you a message this morning that would make a match with what Mrs. Conner has said.

**WHITE:** We were here last February, which time I tried to show the pictures of some historic characters of the area, in which I have been very much interested in recent years.

**WHITE:** I don't know whether this is the same group I addressed at that time or not. I think some of you are, but following that meeting we have become very well acquainted with Mr. and Mrs. John Conner, and found their deep interest in church history in general and the history of the Bethel Church in particular.

**WHITE:** I do not like to start on a note of sarcasm, but I feel like saying that we come from a church in which at present the word “church history” is a dirty word, and any mention of the religious heritage which our community has over where we live seems to be almost a heresy. So it is quite refreshing, I assure you, to come this morning into a church and into a group where I can indulge my interest in church history without being reprimanded for the offence.

**WHITE:** Mrs. Conner, in a recent communication, sent me an outline which she asked me to follow if I could, and I will do so as religiously as I can. It was along the line of some things I had intended to speak to you about anyway, since she invited us to come here some weeks ago.

**WHITE:** Among the things that she asked, the first on the agenda was to say something about the life of my great-great-great-grandfather, James Dinsmore, to whom she has already referred in her introductory remarks.

**WHITE:** James Dinsmore was a Scottish-Irishman about whom we do not know as much as we would like to know, being still a member of the Dinsmore clan.

**WHITE:** My middle name being Dinsmore, I can't evade the fact that I belong to the Dinsmore clan.

**WHITE:** We have annual family reunions, and they are very enjoyable as we get together with others who are descended from this man.

**WHITE:** I have jotted down here some of the things that we do know about James Dinsmore, and I'm going to speak first about his personal life. He was born in County Antrim, near Belfast, on April 26, 1742, and came to America with two or three brothers in 1760 or 1761.

**WHITE:** And in the following winter, probably in the winter of 1761 and 1762, he and his brother Robert crossed the Allegheny Mountains by making snowshoes of splits 75 inches in size.

**WHITE:** When he came here he settled near Pittsburgh, where he lived for a number of years. In 1772 he settled on Miller's Run in the present South Fayette Township — that is, to the southwest a couple of miles from Bridgeville. But the patent for his land there — the Land of Canaan it was called — was not given until 1786.

**WHITE:** He frequently went back east over the mountains to obtain salt, and during the years of the Revolutionary War James Dinsmore served in various capacities, for at least we know that a James Dinsmore is given credit for certain war services, and descendants of our James Dinsmore have joined the DAR and SAR on his war record.

**WHITE:** James Dinsmore was married at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, in 1776 to Miss Rebecca Walker, and it would appear that they lived, at least during a part of the war years, in York or Adams County.

**WHITE:** After the purchase of this tract of land — the Land of Canaan — however, they appear to have lived constantly in South Fayette Township.

**WHITE:** They belonged to the Bethel Presbyterian Church, which James Dinsmore served as elder. In 1791 Rebecca Walker Dinsmore died, and her body was brought for burial to the old graveyard here at Bethel. But her grave has never been marked.

**WHITE:** One day, several years ago, other descendants of James Dinsmore came — we came over here to Bethel and we searched the cemetery over, but found no record, no marker to her grave.

**WHITE:** And I believe that we were told at the headquarters and cemetery that there was no record of her burial. But the tradition of the family is that her body was brought here for burial. On the day of her funeral, the rough coffin was strapped to a pair of farm wagon wheels and thus transported to the graveyard, while James Dinsmore, the husband, and some of his neighbors followed on horseback.

**WHITE:** His son John, then a lad of about 12 years, I think, wanted to follow his mother to her last resting place, but he was told that he could not.

**WHITE:** He intended to walk, so he carefully turned back home.

**WHITE:** In 1795 James Dinsmore sold his farm in South Fayette Township, because it was too far from church — about seven miles — and he purchased a tract known as Huntington Plantation in Canton Township, about six miles from Washington and three miles from Buffalo Village.

**WHITE:** In this new location he transferred his membership in 1795 — I think the records of the church here show that — from the Bethel Church to the Upper Buffalo Church at Buffalo Village, and soon he was elected an elder there, serving that office until his death in 1817.

**WHITE:** In 1800, in a double wedding ceremony, James Dinsmore was married to his third wife, Elizabeth Carr.

**WHITE:** And in the same ceremony, his son, John Dinsmore, was united to her sister, Jane Carr. And then that was a sufficient tie between the Dinsmore and the Carr families. Three years later a daughter, Anna Dinsmore, was married to James Carr, a brother of Elizabeth and Jane. So there were three intermarriages there in a period of three years, and it is rather notable that the father and the son of this Dinsmore family married sisters.

**WHITE:** Their home in the new location was probably a log house, but in 1808 and 1809 bricks were burnt on Huntington Plantation, and in 1810 a large brick house was built — 14 rooms.

**WHITE:** The home of the two families, James Dinsmore and his wife, his son John and his wife and family. And it was the home of James Dinsmore for the rest of his life, and he died in his own house. It is still standing, and is the home of another great-great-great grandson of James Dinsmore, Malcolm Dinsmore, and his wife and family. The whole property is still in the Dinsmore name.

**WHITE:** That is, I think, sufficient about all we know actually about the personal life of James Dinsmore.

**WHITE:** I have two quotations now that I want to give you from [unclear] concerning his saintliness and godliness.

**WHITE:** This says that he was noted for his saintliness and as an eminently pious man of God. He is spoken of as the burning and shining light, a man wonderfully full of the spirit of prayer. When the Bethel Presbyterian Church was organized, he, James Brice, and James Kirkpatrick — three men, I believe the name James in that direction — became its first ruling elders, and he continued in this capacity until he was dismissed to the Upper Buffalo Church in 1795, where he served as an elder the rest of his life.

**WHITE:** As an indication of his standing in the early Presbyterian Church, he was appointed to the Board of Commissioners of the Western Missionary Society of the Synod of Pittsburgh in 1804.

**WHITE:** Another tribute comes from a grandson, the Reverend James H. Dinsmore, a graduate of W&J College, and a man who, although he was not a family man — he was never married — was familiar with the family tradition. And he says this concerning his grandfather, James Dinsmore:

**WHITE:** Grandfather was a farmer and well-to-do, as were most of his male descendants.

**WHITE:** I don't know whether that applies to all his male descendants. I couldn't vouch for that, I felt it a little bit, but he was a large man and very corpulent in his old age. He weighed, according to the family tradition, 300 pounds. He must have been quite a man physically.

**WHITE:** For nearly 50 years he was an honored elder in the Presbyterian Church. For many years — and here's an error — at Montour or Miller's Run, Allegheny County, Pennsylvania. His grandson should have known better than that. But that statement about Miller's Run — plenty of Dinsmores went up to the old Miller's Run Presbyterian Church in Cecil Township, a congregation which is now non-existent. But the old church is still there, the old building, and the graveyard is very historic.

**WHITE:** But some of his people have looked all over that old graveyard for some of the Dinsmore graves, but that is apparently an error, because he was an elder first here, and then for many years at Upper Buffalo — a contemporary with Dr. McMillan, the founder of Jefferson College, and who was also his pastor. That statement also was a little bit of an error, because while I have no doubt he was very well acquainted with Dr. John McMillan, and yet, except that [he was] the organizing pastor here, I don't believe John McMillan would have been his pastor by any chance in the world.

**WHITE:** For the last 25 or 30 years of his life he was an elder in the Upper Buffalo Church in Washington County with those eminent preachers and pioneers, Joseph Smith, Thomas Marquis, John Anderson, and he participated in that wonderful revival in the early part of this century — the great revival of 1802 — when the great camp meeting was held at the Upper Buffalo Church, attended, some say, by 10,000 people who came there from a radius of 100 miles or so, camped on the grounds, and had that great evangelistic [unclear].

**WHITE:** His memory is still precious, and he has left his descendants the unspeakable blessing of an eminently good and very influential Christian, whom the Church failed not to honor. The records of Presbytery show during James Dinsmore's lifetime that he was in attendance at at least 21 meetings of these — both Synod of Pittsburgh and the Presbytery of Redstone, I guess. His son John Dinsmore, my great-great-grandfather, followed his father as an elder in the Upper Buffalo Church, and he served there until his death in 1858.

**WHITE:** What I'm trying to get at in the enumeration here of the good qualities of James Dinsmore is to try to accomplish as much as Mrs. Conner intimated in her introductory remarks — the great influence between the Cross Creek Church and the Bethel Church.

**WHITE:** I wanted to include in a sort of a trilogy the Upper Buffalo Church too, because of the fact that James Dinsmore went to that church — there were elders there, his son and his grandson. James Dinsmore III, son of John, [married] Mary Margaret Lyell, daughter of Robert Lyell, and they were my great-grandparents. About 1830 this couple moved to a farm which John Dinsmore had purchased in Cross Creek Township near Cross Creek Village. And here in 1837, James Dinsmore III, grandson of James I, was elected a ruling elder, to serve the church there until his death in 1870.

**WHITE:** When he went to the Cross Creek Church, it was being served as pastor by the Reverend John Stockton, and Stockton [was] pastor there during the rest of Dinsmore's life — John Stockton.

**WHITE:** There was a pastor there and a word or two should be said about him. He belonged to the famous Stockton family who had come to the Washington, Pennsylvania, area.

**WHITE:** In the late 1700s, early 1800s, John Stockton was a graduate of Washington College in the class of 1820.

**WHITE:** When he came to be pastor of the Cross Creek Church, he brought with him not only his interest in the work of the kingdom, but also he was interested in the education of the young people of his, the young men, I should say, of his congregation.

**WHITE:** In 1827, when he came to Cross Creek Church, the school systems of our area were not very well developed. The Public School Act of 1834 had not been passed, and so the best schools that they had in the various communities were the so-called subscription schools.

**WHITE:** However, to supplement the subscription schools, plenty of communities were developing the so-called academies. And so John Stockton was pastor at the Cross Creek Church only one year when he decided the Cross Creek area needed an academy.

**WHITE:** So he was instrumental in organizing the Cross Creek Academy in 1828, and that existed, [with] some interruptions, until the year 1895, when it became the Cross Creek Township High School. Soon after he came to Cross Creek and had the academy going, he looked around for the best young men from the college classes of Washington College at Washington, Jefferson College at Canonsburg, to find able young men to serve as teachers in the academy.

**WHITE:** And among the fine young men who he secured to teach there — I think during the very early years, he must have been during the early years of the Cross Creek Academy — he employed the young man George Marshall as a teacher in the academy.

**WHITE:** Mr. Marshall was a graduate of Jefferson College, the class of 1831. Well, outside of that, John Stockton had graduated from Washington College in 1820. It was a time when there was a great deal of competition between the two colleges.

**WHITE:** You know, they were eventually united into the present Washington and Jefferson College, but during the years there was a great deal of rivalry. I think sometimes it was bitter too, but at least the graduates of the two institutions were liberal enough to recognize the goodness of the graduates of the other college. So John Stockton, I think, had no compulsion or no revulsion at all against the employment of George Marshall as a teacher in the Cross Creek Academy.

**WHITE:** Well, the tenure of Marshall as a teacher there must have been very short. According to the history of Washington Presbytery, Mr. Marshall was licensed to preach, I think, in the year 1830.

**WHITE:** And he was ordained to the ministry and became a pastor of the Bethel Church here in 1833. So I would give him maybe only two years at the most, not more than three, to have been connected with the Cross Creek Academy. But he did make one other affiliation with the Cross Creek country, which was a lifelong affiliation, in that he married Mary Lee, daughter of Hugh Lee of the Cross Creek family.

**WHITE:** And as I say, he was ordained to the ministry here in 1833. He was its pastor, and he served until his death in 1872.

**WHITE:** During the years of his ministry here, I happen to know that a lasting friendship existed between the two men, Dr. John Stockton, who was, I suppose, about 10 years older than George Marshall. Then the fact that George Marshall was married into the Lee family of Cross Creek — that meant that there was a tie with Cross Creek which never was broken until Dr. Marshall's death.

**WHITE:** He died in 1872, and Dr. Stockton didn't die until about 10 years later.

**WHITE:** He remained a firm friend of John Stockton and frequently returned to the Cross Creek area and asked to greet him at the Cross Creek Church. The notes I have show some of the times that George Marshall preached at the Cross Creek Church as well. On April 2nd, 1854, he preached on the text Exodus 15:26, which reads, “For I am the Lord that healeth thee.” On June 18, 1858, from the text 1 Thessalonians 5:6, “Therefore let us not sleep, as do others, but let us watch and be sober.”

**WHITE:** And then the weekend of September 23, 24, and 25, 1864, during the late years of the Civil War.

**WHITE:** Dr. Marshall spent that weekend in the Cross Creek community and assisted Dr. Stockton in conducting the communion service. You know, the old communion service started usually on Friday with a preparatory service, ended with a second preparatory service on Saturday afternoon.

**WHITE:** The sacrament itself was administered on Sabbath morning. And then, what perhaps some of you do not know, the communion service continued into Monday morning, when a post-communion sermon was preached by a pastor or by a visiting pastor. So this weekend, on September 23, 1864, from the text of Hosea 10:12, Dr. Marshall preached, “Sow yourselves in righteousness, and reap in mercy.”

**WHITE:** And on Saturday afternoon, Luke 15:10 — “Likewise I say unto you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner that repenteth.”

**WHITE:** According to the record, on Sunday morning — that was morning September 25th — he assisted Dr. Stockton with the sacrament.

**WHITE:** On January 28th, 1866, preaching from Revelation 2:5 — “Remember therefore whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works, or else I will come to thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his place, except thou repent.”

**WHITE:** The last record I have of Dr. Marshall's preaching at Cross Creek was on June 23, 1867, which was indicated as the last day, when he preached from Jeremiah 8:22 — “Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?”
