**NARRATOR:** The interview which follows was recorded on July the 15th, a Thursday evening, 1971, at Hickory, Pennsylvania, and is a record of the interview of Mr. A.D. White and his wife by Ann Conner and John Conner. There is a considerable gap from this announcement to the beginning of the interview, so please stand by.

**WHITE:** …and the library historical works and other library books. Is the impression I got at Canonsburg that the people of the town resisted their being taken to W&J College, on account of the feeling that existed between the two towns over the college being taken away from Canonsburg. So whether those books are still in Canonsburg, or whether they've been destroyed or disappeared, I have no knowledge — no direct knowledge. I think Dr. Jim Herron at Canonsburg knows something about those.

**INTERVIEWER:** Do you know him?

**WHITE:** No.

**INTERVIEWER:** Well, that was just one of the many questions that I wanted to ask you.

**WHITE:** Well, I've been down there at the historical room at the old Jefferson College building when things looked pretty neat around there. Well, the few times I was back, I each time showed that the conditions had deteriorated. As I say, it got so bad that it appeared like the wind of the panes knocked out and the birds would get in there and fly around. And the thing just got so bad that, well, of course, the building is torn down now.

**WHITE:** But it is a good question what happened to those old works. I think they were looking into — and I have no knowledge the last several years, but when I inquired perhaps as much as ten years ago, that was a story they told me about. I have material that I've acquired recently. It could be that some of that has been allowed to go to Washington.

**INTERVIEWER:** Well, I intend to go back again there and see what I can find.

**WHITE:** When I was doing research on the Cross Creek church, of course I got most material in the Cross Creek church's own records, but I was at Philadelphia a couple of times consulting the files of the General Assembly. And this particular book — I say I think the William Vance affair first went to General Assembly in 1821. However, if I know that this — the same question had been covered by a volume like this, you read the whole story. Because, see, typing a certain verse in Leviticus, it doesn't apply at all, but the leaders of the Presbyterian Church got the idea that if a man's wife died and he married his deceased wife's sister, that he was committing a crime against the teachings of the Bible.

**WHITE:** They claim, in fact, that it was an incestuous marriage at one time. Then when it came up that William Vance took his case to General Assembly, the only rule that General Assembly would make on it was to refer him back to the station at Crosby. They had ruled that his marriage with his deceased wife's sister was not according to the teachings of the Bible, and so they deprived both of them of the privileges of the church. As long as that couple lived they were never allowed to commune again at Cross Creek Church.

**WHITE:** So I went to see what the records of General Assembly had to say on it, and in 1821 William Vance took an appeal from Washington Presbytery to General Assembly, and it referred back to the session at Cross Creek Church. He wasn't satisfied with that. The next year he was back at General Assembly — was another appeal; they went all over it again, and decided that there was no justification for that ruling otherwise and had the year before. So he didn't get any place, and the rest of his life he went to church but wasn't allowed to protect your community — the same was true of his wife. He lived — he didn't bother to try another denomination, then; he stayed within the church even though he fought this all his life.

**INTERVIEWER:** That's very — it would have been unthinkable, I suppose. It would have been a worse crime to have gone to a senior chancellor. Probably would have been a terrible thing.

**INTERVIEWER:** Could you explain to me — I noticed that among the early pastors, it was always a custom when they had communion to have a guest pastor. Why was this so?

**WHITE:** Well, I wouldn't know why, but it wasn't very — up to the custom. I think almost without fail in Cross Creek Church, Dr. Stockton had a guest minister for the Friday night preparatory, usually the same minister for Saturday afternoon preparatory, and he stayed for the Sunday communion — sacrament, they call it — and then for the Monday morning post-communion service. They had that at Cross Creek, year after year. So the whole communion season there, I think the idea was that they wanted one of their brother to come in, and when Dr. Marshall would come to Cross Creek for one communion season, well, it wouldn't be long until Dr. Stockton was over at Bethel, assisting there. Well, the same was true of John Clark and Dr. McMillan.

**INTERVIEWER:** Yeah. Because that's —

**INTERVIEWER:** The elders were allowed to serve communion at that time, isn't it possible?

**WHITE:** They had very few elders for one time. I never thought of that. No, I don't. I never thought of that. It just seemed to be the idea of a little stronger moral support during that communion season. And quite frequently, the minister who left his own church for that weekend, they didn't have any service there.

**INTERVIEWER:** Yes, that's what I wondered — when they required an assistant, that left the church unattended. And of course, I guess maybe they had a son.

**WHITE:** I got this from Jimmy Simpson diary. Jimmy Simpson was a historian over there during the 19th century. And he kept a daily three-line diary, and it wasn't very much to it usually. But he nearly always had the minister's sermon. And that the minister was not there — he was aware of Moundsville, or Bethel, or Washington — why, he'd say where he was, but the elders would hold society, which was just a sort of a prayer meeting at that time. And by the time that entry would come up, something because the military was away.

**INTERVIEWER:** I see. Well, that was…

**WHITE:** The grandson had crossed, and they're all referring to him. Kept not in very safe condition either, but at Cross Creek, I know there resents very much letting their records go to such places as Pittsburgh or Philadelphia. I don't know. So I thought I would work on them from this standpoint, since I went over all the records of Cross Creek Church pretty thoroughly and making up that book. And I made notes on what I think are the chief actions of both session and trustee nearly all the way through the history of the church.

**WHITE:** Well, I can tell on the idea that I'll give those, my notes, to the church, and then let them send the original records to Philadelphia. They may buy them, I'm not sure. This winter I hope to read the records of Cross Creek Church again to pick up anything that — well, I'm afraid that people ask me or people write to me about their ancestors' names and peers. Well, if I didn't get it in the notes the first time, I might be able to pick up a number of things like that by going over the wreckage again.

**WHITE:** But our church, our local church — the subject of history right now is taboo. We can't do a thing with them. They won't let me do anything. And I don't know what attitude they would take to see our two churches here merge. And they haven't permitted us to have any kind of a historical observance since the merging. They're going to let the records of the two churches go to such a place as Philadelphia. They're not going to do anything with them locally. I think they should be put there for safekeeping. And I don't know — I've been starting to agitate the thing a little bit here, but really I'll get farther with the people of Cross Creek and things of that kind.

**WHITE:** I will hear locally — just present time, because that feeling in connection with the academy at Cross Creek. That's all it has to say about him in connection with the Cross Creek Academy. You have more in the reference you had there.

**WHITE:** No.

**WHITE:** According to the history we have on that, before they built a building especially for the academy, they would use vacant rooms around the homes and the buildings there. **INTERVIEWER:** It has now been torn down.

**WHITE:** But we do have pictures of — I think one of my school now. Yes, and, well, a number of cases, young men studying in the academy could go from that academy to college, maybe skip a year of college and I'd be in another junior class or something like that, get through in just a couple of years.

**WHITE:** The background of this letter is this. General James Lee lived just a mile west of where we are here, right near Mount Prospect Church at this time. And he had grandchildren by the name of Graham. Their parents had died, and two or three of the boys were in the service during the Civil War. But the youngest boy went to George Marshall's to live and go to school. And so when he mentions in the letter Payson, he's referring to this Payson Graham, who was living in his home.

**INTERVIEWER:** And there is the only letter, I think. You know — would you like to read it for us?

**WHITE:** [reading] "This letter is written from Bethel, Pennsylvania, July 27th, 1863, addressed to General James Lee of Hickory, Pennsylvania. My dear friend, on yesterday, Sabbath, just as I was going into the pulpit, I received a note from Pittsburgh informing me of the death of Henry Graham, and wishing me and Payson to attend the funeral. You will see from the time that I received the note the impossibility of my attendance. I am truly sorry that I did not hear of this painful Providence sooner. I would have sent Lee and Payson, but the word came too late. This was a sad occurrence. We never heard of his sickness till the word of his death came.

**WHITE:** [reading] Poor Payson took it very hard. His heart was filled with sorrow, and his eyes became red with weeping. I tried to comfort him. Today he is more cheerful and is in good health. He is a fine boy. We love him very much. There is no school at present, so he has been harvesting some.

**WHITE:** [reading] We want you immediately, in receipt of this, to write and let us know all about Henry — Henry, when he died, of what disease, how long he was sick, and whether he took death to himself. In a word, all you know about his case. Henry was a young man much beloved in this community, one that bid fair for usefulness, but God had otherwise determined. How mysterious his providence.

**WHITE:** [reading] How many of our precious young men have been cut down since this accursed rebellion took place, and it does seem to me that the wrath of the Holy God does and will rest on the leaders of this rebellion. They have filled the land with mourning and sorrow.

**WHITE:** [reading] Please let us know also where he was buried, and what ministers were present. Payson got a letter sometime since from his sister in Steubenville, and expects to answer it this week. We are all in usual health. May God give you all the grace to bear this sore trouble with becoming resignation. He says, 'What I do thou knowest not now, but shalt know hereafter.' Soon we shall know the reason of all our trials, and praise God forever for them. Our love to you all. Yours very gratefully, George Marshall."

**WHITE:** [reading] "P.S. Let me know whether they are likely to have any trouble from raids in your region and about Cross Creek and the draft. Direct to Upper St. Clair Post Office."

**WHITE:** Well, that was a summer. 1863 was the summer that Morgan, the Raider, got up into Ohio. And so, of course, they got very much exercise about them all through the Cross Creek country. But fortunately, he got up into the region there, back of East Liverpool, Ohio, and was captured. And so that's what he was referring to there.

**INTERVIEWER:** That's probably what he was referring to there.
