# Bethel Presbyterian Church

## Historical Room - Oral History, 25 September 1983

**Ruth Valentine · Miller Maits · Jean Snowden | recorded 25 September 1983 | 26 m**

---

> **Provenance.** Recorded 25 September 1983 (date supplied by the family; the tape's own 'almost 206 or 207 years' since the 1776 founding agrees). FOUR voices: Ruth Valentine, Miller Maits, Jean Snowden, plus an unnamed moderator labelled HOST who introduces each speaker and reads the church history. The three speaker surnames were confirmed by the family and do not all appear on the tape: Ruth's own cradle-roll certificate names her Ruth Elizabeth Douglas (b. 26 December 1913), so Valentine is a married name; MAITS is the family spelling of a surname the tape pronounces 'Mates'; Jean is given on tape only as 'Jean'. The HOST remains unidentified. Ruth and Jean are acoustically very close (206 vs 208 Hz), so the speaker split rests on the moderator naming each at handover, not on voice matching alone. One judgement call is flagged at 15:47-16:52. Corrections include 'Train up a child in the way he should go' (Proverbs 22:6, read from a certificate), Reiter, Wycoff, Canonsburg, and missionary society. Eleven items remain unresolved and are left as heard.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** I was born in this section and lived here all my life. I was born in Fort Tidball. They called it Fort Tidball. It was off Logan Road, below the church. And the bricks were made at the same time as the house that Reverend Marshall lived in. But what I thought, since this was supposed to be a Sunday school class, that perhaps you'd be interested suggested, in the cradle roll picture. Now, I don't think that is given anymore to members of the cradle roll.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** Now, I was born in December, and this says cradle roll certificate, and they brought young children to him, and he took them up in his arms, put his hands upon them and blessed them. This certifies that Ruth Elizabeth Douglas was enrolled as a member of the cradle roll of Bethel Sunday School of Bethel Presbyterian on the 28th day of March, 1914, born on the 26th day of December, 1913, signed by Mary Wycoff, Superintendent, and Henry Harley, or Mary Wycoff, superintendent of the cradle roll and Henry Harley superintendent and it says Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** So then, at that time, I wasn't even baptized when they gave the cradle roll because my mother had written on the back, right here, baptized. Now I think I must have been one of the first that Reverend Reiter baptized. He was installed in May in 1914 and I was baptized in June 1914. Then, I was promoted from the cradle roll to the beginner's department, and that was September 1917. In 1917, the beginner's department, I remember just mostly of the little wood chairs that we used, and Mrs. Reiter taught the class, and we met where the stairway up to the sanctuary is.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** They called the fire tire uh it was just a small little room and then there was steps up and there's a room above it uh this was dated 1917 signed by Mary Wycoff Henry Harley and Murray C. Reiter and then I have a certificate of promotion of Bethel Presbyterian Sunday School. This certifies Ruth Douglas as qualified to be promoted from the primary to the junior department. And it was 1923. Elsie McConkie and Mr. Kravitz was superintendent. Then this was from the intermediate department to the senior department.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** And Mrs. Hamilton S. Brown was Superintendent of the Intermediate Department, and H. E. Pellett was Superintendent. And this was in 1929 when I was in high school. But one of the things that mentioned Mrs. Brown, we had lightbearers, and it was mostly young people, and I think, I don't know how often we met. I can't remember much about what we accomplished, but I know one thing that was sponsored by the Lightbearers was the Tom Thumb Wedding. If you can imagine taking charge of about 30 youngsters under seven and getting them to perform.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** Now, I wasn't in the Tom Thumb wedding, Miller Maits back there was, and his sister was maid of honor in the Tom Thumb wedding, and it was really well done, I think. I mean, that was the impression I had. I was one of the older ones, and wasn't a member of the cast, but I had help with the reception afterwards. And then another thing that I remember very distinctly was the Christian Endeavor and I didn't attend regularly. I went once in a while and we had picnics and so forth.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** I always managed to get the picnics but Sunday evening sometimes there was other things that need done and they had plays and I can remember the one play in particular nothing but the truth. I imagine some of you are familiar with it but it is uh it was surprising to me how hard it was to go 24 hours without saying anything that was not true. And that was what the theme of the thing was. And another one was Home Came Ted.

**RUTH VALENTINE:** And Isabel Hemlinger coached the plays and then they performed them in the Bethel High School.

**HOST:** Thank you, Ruth. Again, we're open to any questions as each person speaks. Feel free to raise some questions. The next person is Miller Maits. And Miller, as I mentioned earlier, is a descendant of the Oliver Miller family who lived in the homestead down below. So he comes from a long, historic family. And he and his family have also been active and dedicated members of Bethel for many, many years.

**MILLER MAITS:** Public speaking is a little out of my line. I know. They're among friends. My remarks aren't so much related to the church as they are to the place here. I joined the church, I think, at the age of about 14. I still remember. As a small boy, here in the old place, a couple of my outstanding memories of being entertained here at the old home, playing with a collection of old arrowheads, Indian relics and one thing or another turned up in the ground right in this area below us here every time they plowed apparently was an old encampment when Indians were traveling back and forth.

**MILLER MAITS:** There's a spring there; it's still there. Another memory of childhood days here. As you come up from the circle, we cross a concrete bridge today. Years back was an old wooden span. I might have set in there five, six years old. Old piece of string and a bent pin on it for a piece of hook sitting there fishing over off that old bridge. In fact, I grew up around it. I've been up here all my life.

**HOST:** I'll tell you a little bit about some of the churches. I think we've called on everybody who's here to ask to speak, and there are some other people with historic backgrounds here that may want to step up and say a few words and I hope you will. Let me tell you a little bit about our churches and I'll check our time to make sure we don't get into the church service. We still have about 15 minutes. The first church after the Miller homestead, the church had started there in 1779.

**HOST:** The first church was built and there was a log structure which was built right across from our present cemetery on the other side of the road, not the side of the road where our church building is now. And that lasted until 1826 when it was torn down and we replaced it with a brick structure for the first time. And that was built on the present site of our church building. In 1855, we built another church. You can see here we were always building churches.

**HOST:** And every 40 or 50 or 100 years we build another church. That was built from some of the bricks of the Second Church. There's a kind of historical connection between the bricks of one church and the bricks of another. And, in fact, the bricks in our present church, which was built in 1910, some of those came from the Second Church and the Third Church. So, historically, we have even the bricks kind of carry through our history through the ages. It was kind of interesting that on Easter Sunday, a little bit prior to 1910, I'm trying to think of the exact date now, I think I have it here someplace, but 1909, yeah I guess it was 1909, Edna Croco, who was unable to be here this morning because she's not too well, was going to tell us this story, I understand, that she was in the church service at that time, and they were having Easter Sunday service, and just as the minister was getting up to preach the sermon, a tornado sprung up and took the roof off the church, scared the people to death, naturally, and everybody rushed out and tried to find safety, but, of course, a tornado, there is no safety.

**HOST:** Fortunately, the tornado passed and went on, but as a result of that part, at least, they decided to build our church finally, so we replaced that church. That was one of the rather exciting things that happened along the way. It's kind of interesting to know also that up through Mr. Yarnell's pastorate, which was up through 1960, I forget when he left, we had only had six pastors in that whole period of time. That was for the first 175 years of our history, we had only six pastors in our church.

**HOST:** And that was a rather unusual record. Each pastor would stay for 35 or 40 years. Today, that's not the custom in hardly any churches. Most pastors move on after six or seven years. One of the other important bits of history, and I'm going to ask Jean to tell us a little bit about the women of the church. They have the most interesting story from the church, I think, of anybody. In 1794, a pastor at that time was John Clark. He was an aged man at that time.

**HOST:** He was up in years. This was the time the Whiskey Rebellion had started. It started in this community, as you know, and there were about, I think about 500 men came from various areas around principally Canonsburg and even down into this area and they were going to burn down the house of General Neville who was leading the government forces at that time and was trying to enforce the whiskey tax laws and Pastor Clark tried to dissuade them from doing this pointing out that this was a new government that it was more important to obey the government even if the laws were at that point unjust that they could change the law through the legal processes and should not try to change it through force of arms.

**HOST:** He was unable to dissuade them, however. They attacked General Neville's house, which was over in Bower Hill, incidentally, and his forces killed Major McFarlane, who was the major in charge of that group. Pastor McMillan, John McMillan, who founded our church, never was a pastor of our church. He preached and served at the Hill Church, which is out on Route 19, as you know, near the approach. Donaldson's Crossroads. He preached there from 1775. He started in 1775 and preached for 55 years, which is a lot into 1830.

**HOST:** He also preached at another church near there called Pigeon Creek. Our church, as far as building is concerned, a couple of points that may be of interest to you. Up until 1947, I believe it was, we did not have a very large basement in our church, and we excavated one of the big projects we did was to excavate the basement at that time. Let me tell you a little bit about, I think I have some of the costs here. When our third church building was built in 1855, it cost us $6,000.

**HOST:** We built the second church, or we built this church, the fourth church in 1910. It cost us $30,000 to build our present church. We built the parsonage across the way from that in 1874 for $3,200. When we excavated the basement, it cost us $55,000 to $60,000 just to excavate the basement of a church for which we paid $30,000 to build in 1910. We built an addition on our church in 1951, cost us $247,000, and the last addition, as I remember the figures, came out to about $400,000.

**HOST:** So you can see how prices have escalated and costs of our church have escalated. Another little bit of interest, I think, is the oldest grave in the cemetery near our church dates back to 1784, the grave of Elizabeth Kitto, wife of James Kitto, one of the early Revolutionary soldiers. And I think I'm going to turn this over to Gene for a few minutes. In preface, I might say that in 1947, we elected our first woman deacon. 1963, we elected our first woman elder.

**HOST:** So we've been very, very conservative from the standpoint of using the talents of women in our church. Maybe conservative isn't the right word. Backward, perhaps, is a better word. Hidebound, maybe is even better. And so we just began to come to life in the last relatively few years. Another little point. I keep seeing something more. Our Samuel Fife, who was a long-time elder of our church and goes clear back to and descending from John McMillan also, I believe, is responsible, I'm told, for planting most of the trees in Bethel in the early days.

**HOST:** He was a farmer. He had a great feeling about trees, and he had a lot to do with when we started to build homes and so forth of having trees built around those homes. That's something I have not been aware of. Jean, why don't you take over and tell us about some of the women's activities.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** It's very typical that men don't want the women to have a say. The present women's organization is the United Presbyterian Women, and all the women of the church are united in that. But through the years, there were three different organizations: the Ladies Aid Society. And one of the first accounts that I know of that the women were active was in 1785. They were called upon to help dress the seminary students. So they made summer clothes out of linen that they dyed with new mown hay.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** I don't know what color that would turn out to be. And then they sent their — I don't know what that means either. And then the material was sent back to them, and they actually made the suits for the seminary students. They got into many more activities and the women were always busy doing something. When the building that is the current building was built in 1910, the women had a lot of work to do. They wanted to make money for that project.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** So they put on chicken dinners. And one lady even donated 11 chickens, which was an enormous donation in that day. You know, Edna Croco told us at the UPW meeting a couple of weeks ago that they put on oyster suppers as well. So they were busy. Some of the things the Ladies' Aid Society did, they provided the church furniture, helped with the church repairs, helped with the decorating. They bought tablecloths and dishes and silverware and window blinds for the parsonage and hymn books.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** The Christian flag, the steam table, church linoleum and screens. They contributed to the organ fund. Does that sound familiar to today's work for the women? We're still doing it. So there was the Ladies' Aid Society. Then some of the women were more interested in missionary societies. We usually think of missionary societies being women's groups, but back in the beginning the first missionary society was organized in 1822. And of the 106 members, only 44 were ladies, and the men held all the offices.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** So they kept the requirement for membership was that you either donate 50 cents or goods equaling 50 cents. So they continued for quite a while and in 1840 the women decided that they had to have their own missionary society, they had their own officers except for the treasurer and the minister, Dr. Marshall said he had to take care of the money and then the other society was the Fannie Dietrich Society, which was started in 1887, and it was a group of young women who were interested in missionary work.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** During the years, much money was gathered, worked for, and sent to missions. We are still carrying on that tradition. In 1945, the three groups were joined in one then to make our current UPW organization, and we are still carrying on the traditions of missionary work, the traditions of service to the church, and the traditions of service to outsiders. Dave and I are comparatively new members. We've only been members for 33 years, but we have seen the last two phases of the building since we came.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** When we came, the auditorium wing was not built, and the other wing with the chapel. The Learning Center and the offices and so forth weren't built, but the church building was crowded. The room which is now the choir room was the kindergarten room for four and five-year-olds, and in that tiny room there were as many as 70 kids each Sunday. So you know why we had to build the little children. They met in the kitchen because there was no other place for them.

**JEAN SNOWDEN:** When we came, our youngest was a year and a half, and that's where she ended up in the kitchen. So we have made progress. We can't sit or stand on our path, but we have to go forward.

**HOST:** I see. I read the record of what the women did over the years. You wonder just why they stood by so many years and had no offices in the church. But at that time, it was a practice not to give women offices of responsibility. And it's a terrific waste of talent and persons that you can see over the years. As Gene points out, and as we bring our little meeting to a close, we'd like to say that we've talked a lot about the past, but really, as far as Bethel is concerned, we want to look toward the future.

**HOST:** We do have, we feel, a great future ahead of us with over 200 years now of history, almost 206 or 207 years, I guess, this year. It is time for us then to turn our views outward and upward and think in terms of what Bethel can and should be doing in this day and age. And so we'd like to leave you on that note that we hope that you agree with us that Bethel has a great future. And that we are glad to be part of that at this time.

**HOST:** Thanks.
