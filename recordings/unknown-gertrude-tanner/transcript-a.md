**HOST:** This is the 22nd of [May], 1985, in the library room of the Bethel Presbyterian Church, with Gertrude Tanner.

**HOST:** And she's going to give us a little bit of her early beginnings in Bethel, and the beginnings are also on the side of the women's club.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** You ladies have asked me to talk about looking back about the history of the club and of the area here, and I can start by telling you that old Fort Couch Road was an area here where Indians evidently came, and they had to have a fort along that rushing stream that came alongside of those garages that are existing down there now.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** They needed fresh water, so they had to build a fort by the stream. And Mr. Couch, or Mr. Couch, must have given the land for the fort, because they named the fort Couch.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now later, he had a son who was going to marry by the name of Nathan.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And he gave Nathan a piece of ground and maybe a whole farm for all I know, but at least he gave him a place where he built a log cabin and later a stone house. Now that stone house still exists and was part of the Martin farm. So the Martins built a large addition, a frame addition, onto the stone place.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now the Otts lived there on Kennebec Road, and that was probably a little lane that came up from the fort where his son and family were living.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, this fort had to date back as far as 1750, I would judge. I don't think it could be further back than that, but at least I would say that, because the church was organized, the Bethel Church, over in the stone manse, what was the stone house there. The church was organized in about 1776, is that about right?

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, people in those days had to travel by horseback or wagon to get to these different places, but they certainly must have gone to the Bethel Church over at this old stone land. And later, a church was built on Bethel Hill, not far from where this fort had been, but probably was torn down by this time.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** The reason that I know about Nathan Couch and his having lived there on the Martin Farm was because I had come here as a bride in 1923 and had come out to see my husband's brother who was living on Brookside Boulevard.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** It seems that in the early days, around 1900, when they talked of putting a streetcar or an interurban from Castle Shannon to Washington, PA, the Freehold Real Estate in Pittsburgh had a bright idea that they would buy up parcels of land, and whole farms, in an area that was called, that they later named Brookside. They named it Brookside Farm.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Well, naturally, with the fort having been right across the road, they named all the streets Indian names. It was Kennebec, Arapahoe, Seneca, and so on.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** But strangely enough, they took the main one of the rain roads that went from north to south and called it a boulevard. It was anything but an elegant boulevard, but it was a road.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, my husband's brother built a house in the early development of Brookside, and if you came down Mohawk Road and you didn't turn right or left on the boulevard, you would run right into this house. I don't know who lives there today, but that was the house in the spring of 1923 that we got in our Model T Ford and came out to call on Howard.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And when I saw Brookside in the spring, I knew that this was the place that I would love. And I said, oh, if I could only live here, I'd be happy. Well, strangely enough, on the other side of the interurban tracks and the stream, was part of this Martin Farm.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And there on the side, on the lower side of Sylvan Road, were two houses with great big porches facing Brookside.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** They were on the opposite side of the roadside, and I don't think they were called verandas in that space. Now a veranda was more than a porch - a porch could be small, but these ran the whole width of the house.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And you put rugs on them, and you put tables and an oil lamp or whatever, and you sat out there away from the dust of the road, and you had this gorgeous view of Brookside in the spring, fall.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** In the spring, they had not cut down trees like most builders do today. They had even planted them. They had planted a white pear tree that was white in the spring, called a Kieffer pear.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now the Kieffer pear, to eat it out of your hand, unless it was dead ripe, it was a grainy pear. But if you cooked it, and I canned many a quart of Kieffer pears, they were just delicious.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** I can't look at you or I'll say something.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** This was what I was introduced to.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** These two big houses that were facing Brookside had carriage houses. Now they had come out here long before this interurban had been developed. They had come out here to the country as their summer home probably, and they had a place for the carriages and a place for the horses underneath. And they, of course, later became garages.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** One of them had the top torn off, where somebody - whether they stored grain there, I don't know.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** But the second house, the one that I was able to rent, was built right on the road, and you would wheel the carriage in on the lower level, but it had a second level where they stored the oats for the horses and probably had a place for a man to sleep. But the horses were taken down the side of the road of the house, and the house underneath them, what would be the cellar. Now that's what Charles and I rented on August 1st of 1923. Now because we were young - I'd been born in Scranton, Pennsylvania.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Charles had been an officer in the First World War - but we had very little experience with houses.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Little did we know that pigeons loved to follow horses, and where there's any grain, there are pigeons.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** So that was our first job, was to clean out the rafters where the horses were kept. Then we had to air the bedroom upstairs where the grain had been kept, because if you have oats, and if it's damp weather, those oats can smell pretty terrible.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** So my father came out in his Model T Ford to help me clean this carriage house.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** We lived there two years when our first child was coming, and then we loved that hillside so much, and the view of Brookside, that we bought a chunk out of the next farm, which was the Walden Farm.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And I have lived there 60 years, plus the two in the little carriage house, making 62 years. And I have never moved in a moving van. We had nothing when we came to the carriage house but a cedar chest and a trunk, which went on the back of the Ford. We carried everything else across the road. So I never moved.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, the war was over - the First World War to end all wars was over - and building now started up again. Some houses had been built before the war, but without getting materials you couldn't build any.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And all the war effort was taking everybody's interest. So as soon as '22 and '23 came, or when I came to the area, the houses began to spring up. And after the Second World War, which was 20 years about later, we had been so established in the area, and because of the Depression, Charles had had to go into business for himself as an engineer, and we were interested in buying land.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, Charles was - he built dams, earth dams, for the state of Pennsylvania. And I love to tell people that one of the things that I feel is a lovely memorial for anybody's efforts all through their life was the dam and recreation park over there at down the line here, I'm kind of thinking right now, that big lake in the Beaver County, Raccoon Park. I couldn't say the word Raccoon Park. Charles designed that, built it, and built the roads around as his construction company.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Then he also built the one up at Shawnee Lake, which is outside of Bedford, Pennsylvania. And thousands upon thousands of people are there in the hot weather using those facilities. So my job was to drive the car around these country roads while he looked for the site for the dam.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And those are things that I feel are a real memorial for our family to remember. Now, because he was building these dams, he'd lose his men - they'd go to other jobs if he didn't keep them busy. And so we were able to buy what was left of the Martin farm. That was the house, and it was the house, the stone part, that Nathan Couch had built.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And it included what would be a small area, a small farm, which was no longer farmed. The two daughters, Margaret and Almeda, were still living there when we bought it.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, we didn't want that house - we already had a house of our own - but we had to sell it. People wanted to buy it. Here was a very old house with great big deep windowsills for the stone part of it. And they wanted to buy it, but they couldn't get a mortgage. Here was a house, the floor was right on the ground.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Great big wide oak beams lay right on the dirt, and the walls were plastered with mud, horse hair, and lime.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now over those years, they had begun to disintegrate. So the bank said, we do it, and we gut it, the whole thing down to the straight stones, re-plastered, put a foundation in it, and then built it up again.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** And that exists today, and the Otts own it and live in it. And I like to think of those years when Nathan was there, maybe in a log cabin.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, if Nathan were to come back and see what's going on today, he would be horrified, because Fort Couch Road is going to be - instead of a little trail that it once was, and then a dirt road - it's now going to be a four-lane highway. Three lanes, with a lane taking off to the various places that have to take off from a three-lane easily. So it's partly four and partly three-lane highway.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** I like to go back now a little to tell you ladies about the club, but I'll make that a little shorter because you can read the minutes. Here's the minutes book that I have right here. You can read the detail, but I will tell you briefly that how the club started

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** way back in 1925, and it's exactly 60 years old today.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** We had, living in Brookside, first before the club was started there was a book club - I had to mention that - but people were conscious of getting together to socialize, so they were partly groups that got together for friendship as well as to read a book or have a book review.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** But Mrs. Riggs organized the first book club, and from that, three or four other book clubs took off when one would get too large. And I don't know if any exist today or not, but we would buy a book and have to review it. That was good training for all of us.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, next year, in '25, was when Mrs. Edna Cully came to live in Brookside. And she is the one who thought we should have a women's club.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** She probably had come from an area where there was one, because she knew well how to organize it, and to write bylaws, and to get people interested.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** It was really organized to introduce people to each other - people that were coming in but wouldn't know their next-door neighbor well. And the club was to socialize as well as do a little civic work. That came later.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, I was the only charter member left, but that's why they asked me to talk today. And I can say that there was a time when this club was very flourishing.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** We had a drama department. We always had a garden department. Today's garden department is part crafts, but we do call it garden department. We had a musical choral group, and was quite active. In fact, the club was, well, we'll say maybe 25 years ago, a very active group.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** Now, the men had a civic organization even before the women were organized. They would meet at the Brookside trolley stop to go into work in the city and discuss the conditions. And probably through their efforts, we may have gotten streetlights and maybe the street signs were better put up.

**GERTRUDE TANNER:** But it was the women's club - it still exists, and that is what we're celebrating today: 60 years of history right here in Brookside Farms.
